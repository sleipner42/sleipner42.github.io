Split the rent

Pick one method, put in the monthly rent and the people, and read off what each person pays. The three methods answer three different questions, and the middle one is the method our housemates guide recommends for rooms of different sizes.

This calculator needs JavaScript, which is switched off in your browser. Nothing is broken — all three methods are written out in full under how to do it by hand, and each one is a few steps of arithmetic you can do on any phone calculator.

Method

The currency choice only sets the symbol and the number of decimal places. There is no conversion and there are no exchange rates here. Areas are never converted either: measure every room in the same unit as the floor area.

Which method to use

Decide the method before anyone moves in, and write it down. Almost every rent argument between housemates is really an argument about which of these three questions the household agreed to answer.

The three methods, what each one assumes, and when it is the wrong one.
MethodAssumesUse it whenAvoid it when
EquallyEveryone gets the same value out of the homeThe rooms are genuinely comparable, or the household prefers simplicity to precisionOne room is half the size of another, or somebody has an en-suite
By room sizePrivate space is priced, shared space is sharedRooms differ noticeably in size and you can measure themThe rooms are the same size — you will produce a rounding argument for nothing
By incomeThe household has agreed to redistributeEveryone volunteers the figure and everyone agrees to review itAnyone is uncomfortable disclosing pay, or incomes change often

Room size and income are not mutually exclusive in practice: plenty of households price the rooms by area and then let one person take the smallest room because they earn the least. That is the same thing as choosing who gets which room, and it is a much easier conversation than adjusting the arithmetic.

How to do it by hand

None of this needs a computer, and it is worth knowing the method so you can check any calculator, including ours. The one test that matters at the end of all three: add the shares up and see whether you get the rent.

Equally

  1. Add the rent and any shared bills together to get the amount the household has to produce this month.
  2. Divide by the number of people and round down to whole pennies. That is everybody's base share.
  3. Deal with what is left. Multiply the base share by the number of people and subtract it from the total. What remains is a few pennies, and one penny goes to each of that many people.

By room size, with the shared space split equally

  1. Measure each private room and find the total floor area of the whole home. Use one unit throughout — square metres or square feet, it makes no difference as long as it is the same one.
  2. Work out the shared area: total floor area minus all the private rooms added together. That is the kitchen, the bathroom, the hall and the living room.
  3. Work out the rent per unit of area: the rent divided by the total floor area.
  4. Charge each person for their own room at that rate, and add an equal share of the shared area at the same rate: the shared area divided by the number of people, times the rate.
  5. Round down and hand out the leftover pennies as in step 3 above, then check the shares add up to the rent.

The common mistake is to skip straight to dividing the whole rent in proportion to bedroom area. That charges people for the kitchen in proportion to their bedrooms, which nobody would agree to if you said it out loud.

By income share

  1. Add everybody's monthly income together.
  2. Each person's share is the rent times their income divided by the household income.
  3. Round down and hand out the leftover pennies as before.

This is a household choice and not a fairness rule, and it is worth saying so plainly before anybody discloses a payslip. A common softer version is to split only the part of income above a personal floor — what people need for everything that is not rent — which is set out in splitting rent unevenly. Partners with very different pay may also want the equal-leftover rule, compared with this one in splitting costs when you earn different amounts.

Two worked examples you can check

A 90 m² flat at £1,920 for three, priced by room size

The rooms are 16 m², 12 m² and 10 m², so the private space is 38 m² and the shared space is 90 − 38 = 52 m². The rent per square metre is £1,920 ÷ 90 = £21.3333, and each person's equal slice of the shared space is 52 ÷ 3 = 17.3333 m².

The same flat with £150.00 of bills, split equally

Broadband, water and electricity come to £150.00 a month. A bigger room is worth more rent but it does not use more broadband, so the bills go in equally: £50.00 each. Ada pays £761.11, Ben £675.78, Cleo £633.11, and the four figures add up to £2,070.00, which is the rent plus the bills to the penny.

Heating in a house where somebody is away half the month is the honest exception, and it is better split by person-days than by room size or by anything else: the method is in splitting rent and bills with housemates.

Four things this calculator will not do for you

  • Price an en-suite, a balcony or a parking space. Those are premiums, and the way that works is that the person who has it pays the premium and it is rebated equally to everyone who does not, so the premium column sums to zero. Worked through in splitting rent unevenly.
  • Pro-rate a mid-month move-in. Use a daily rate on that person's own room share and the days in that actual month, not a flat half.
  • Settle up who actually owes whom once one person has paid the whole thing to the landlord. That is a different calculation: the bill split calculator takes a list of who paid what and gives you the fewest payments that clear it.
  • Tell you what the law says. This is practical arithmetic between housemates, not legal advice. On a joint tenancy you are each liable for the whole rent whatever you agreed internally, and an internal split is no defence against the landlord.

Common questions

How much rent should each roommate pay?
There is no single right answer, but there is a defensible default: each person pays for their own private room and an equal share of everything the household uses together. Work out the rent per square metre of the whole home, charge each person for the area of their own room at that rate, then divide the cost of the shared area equally. Equal shares are the right answer when the rooms are genuinely comparable, and splitting by income is a choice the household can make rather than a rule.
Why not just divide the rent by the size of each bedroom?
Because that charges people for the kitchen, the bathroom and the living room in proportion to their bedrooms, and nobody uses the kitchen in proportion to their bedroom. Dividing the whole rent by bedroom area alone makes the big room pay for a share of the shared space it does not have. Charge the private rooms by area and split the shared area equally instead.
Is splitting rent by income fair?
It is a household choice, not a fairness rule. Nobody is entitled to know what anyone else earns, the shares move every time someone changes job, and the person paying more does not get more room for it. Some households, especially couples and long-standing shares, choose it deliberately and are happy with it. If you do use it, agree in writing when it gets reviewed.
Is anything I type into this calculator sent anywhere?
No. The calculator is a few lines of JavaScript inside the page itself, so the rent, the room sizes and any incomes you type stay in your browser and are worked out on your own device. There is no analytics, no cookie, no advertising script and no request back to us carrying your numbers, and nothing is stored between visits. Close the tab and it is gone.
How do the shares add up to the rent exactly?
Every share is rounded down to a whole penny first, which leaves a few pennies short of the rent. Those leftover pennies are handed out one each to the people whose exact share was cut by the most, so the column adds up to the rent to the penny. The calculator names who takes an extra penny rather than quietly rounding the rent itself up or down.
Should the bills be split the same way as the rent?
Usually not. A bigger room is worth more rent, but it does not use more broadband, so the calculator splits the shared bills equally by default and lets you switch that off. The honest exception is heating and electricity in a house where people are away for long stretches, which is better split by person-days than by anything to do with the rooms.
